Chinese Queer Corpus

A research corpus of Mandarin speech, gender, and sexuality

The Chinese Queer Corpus (CQC) is a research corpus of Mandarin speech from publicly available recordings of speakers who have described themselves, in their own words, as members of gender or sexual minority communities.

Identity is not a sound.

CQC documents variation in speech. It is not a dataset for classifying people. Our analyses show wide variation within every group and large overlap between groups, and using the corpus to infer anyone’s identity from their voice is prohibited.

Why CQC

Research on language, gender, sexuality, and the voice has produced a substantial body of work, but most of it concerns English and a small number of other languages. Many large Mandarin speech corpora provide limited information about gender and generally do not include detailed self-described sexuality or gender-diversity metadata. CQC provides a resource for studying how these relationships are negotiated in Mandarin Chinese.

CQC is designed for questions such as:

  • How does phonetic variation in Mandarin relate to gender, and how much does it vary within groups?
  • How do speakers use linguistic resources to present and negotiate identity?
  • How does speaking style change across interactional contexts?
  • How do locally meaningful Chinese identity labels relate to linguistic practice?
  • Which findings from English-based research hold for Mandarin, and which do not?

What CQC does not claim

CQC does not assume that:

  • queer speakers share a single way of speaking;
  • gender identity determines how someone speaks;
  • sexual orientation can be identified from the voice;
  • acoustic differences correspond directly to identity categories;
  • its speakers represent Mandarin-speaking queer communities as a whole.

CQC is a corpus for studying variation, not for defining a “queer voice.”

Questions

What is a speech corpus?

A speech corpus is a collection of recorded speech, organized and annotated for research. In CQC, speech is transcribed, and each word and sound is time-stamped so that researchers can measure pronunciation, pitch, and timing.

Why is audio not publicly available?

The source videos were public, but collecting them into a labelled research corpus creates a different context of use: it can make speakers easier to find and to analyze systematically. Public availability does not by itself make redistribution appropriate or unrestricted. Collecting recordings into a labelled research corpus can make speakers easier to locate and analyze systematically.

Who is included?

Adults who described themselves as members of a gender or sexual minority community in their own public videos on Bilibili or YouTube. We record only what speakers said about themselves. We never infer identity from voice or appearance, and we do not use what others have said about them.

Why does self-description matter?

Identity categories are social and cultural. Speakers use many terms to describe themselves, and those terms do not always map neatly onto predefined categories, so CQC keeps each speaker’s own words alongside any analytic category.

I think my video is in CQC. What can I do?

You can ask us to remove it at any time, without giving a reason. See how to request removal or the 中文说明.

I am not a linguist. Can I use CQC?

Yes. Researchers in sociology, gender and sexuality studies, anthropology, communication, and related fields can apply. See For Researchers.
